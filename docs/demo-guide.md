# Synapse demonstration

## Launch

From the repository root, run `python tools/serve.py` and open <http://localhost:8766>. The original rhythm game is at <http://localhost:8766/rhythm.html>. The optional [legacy RPS page](../legacy.html) preserves the frozen-policy game, older quantized neural-message demo, and detailed historical results. Use a local server because the page loads saved data and the local Three.js modules. Python 3 and a current WebGL-capable browser with JavaScript modules are required. The demo has no bundler or external runtime dependency; Three.js is vendored in `vendor/three/`, so it works offline. Keep the entire repository together, especially `vendor/`, `index.html`, `js/`, `fonts/`, `data/subgraph.json`, `data/brain_points.bin`, `data/rps_contextual_bandit.json`, the five `data/rps_action_preference_{teaching_v3,confirmation_first,confirmation_second,strategy_b_initial,strategy_b_confirmation}_*.json` archives, `data/rps_teacher_specific_speed_initial.json`, `data/rps_teacher_guided_matched_veto.json`, and `data/rps_teacher_guided_reset_preflight.json`.

If port 8766 is occupied, run `python -m http.server 8767` from the repository root and open <http://localhost:8767>. Do not open `index.html` as a `file://` URL: browser fetches for the archived JSON and connectome files may fail. Keep `data/rps_connectome_contribution_ablation.json` alongside the existing archives for the neural-versus-digital panel.

## Primary judge screen

The page has three compact tabs on the right (a bottom bar on narrow screens). **Simulation** (the default) holds the live Challenge; the hero's main button, **Watch the 90-second story**, plays the recorded walkthrough; **Stats** holds the archived 15-run result; **Research** holds the separate Strategy A/B five-arm Research Evidence. Fly A's rotatable connectome is in Step 2; the two Fly B connectomes and live graphs are in Step 3. The Three.js point clouds use measured 3D cell-body positions; their light and shadow are a display aid. Before live training they show anatomy only; during the separate live Challenge, actual recent simulator spikes flash in each fly's own view. Archived results do not animate these views: the selected student's fixed first message-bearing row instead shows Fly A's recorded preference, Fly B's three recorded MBON rates, measured DN counts, decoded preference, and the reward/imitation updates. Per-cell archived spike events and per-trial Fly A Q scores were not saved, so the page does not fabricate a replay.

Choose any of six archived students for the exact **five-arm frozen, teacher-disconnected** learning curve. The live Challenge now runs the same action-preference protocol as a demonstration. The older proposal/veto curves, Neural Advice Diagnostic, and Research Replay moved to [archive.html](../archive.html); their failed gates remain valid.

The practical question is whether independently trained agents can share useful learned information through a constrained channel. Normal equipment operation, overheating, and excessive vibration are examples of a **possible future sensor-classification benchmark**. No such task has been implemented, deployed, or measured in Synapse.

## Optional four-step live Challenge

1. **Set up the game.** Pick the opponent from one plain menu: a circle (Rock → Paper → Scissors, preset Strategy A, `next = (previous + 1) mod 3`), the reverse circle (preset Strategy B, `+2`), the experimental Noisy circle (80% rule), or your own repeating pattern of 1-5 moves. A preview shows what the opponent plays and the move that beats it. The opponent never sends its rule, pattern position, or next move to a fly. If one previous move has several possible successors, the page warns that the one-move-memory fly cannot always be right. The noisy task has no established teacher-specific benefit. Choose Fly A's training length (24, 48, 96, 144, or 288 rounds; 288 matches the study) and Fly B's (the same lengths, or **6 rounds, test, then continue to 288**). **Quick Fly B run** skips the in-between frozen tests. Fly A defaults to **288 rounds** and Fly B to **24 rounds**; both can be changed before training. The deterministic seed is under **Advanced**.
2. **Train Fly A.** Click **Train Fly A live**. Its own simulated connectome and three-MBON contextual bandit train from game rewards in 24-round episodes for the chosen length, with its live brain shown under Step 2. It then runs 72 frozen rounds. All three Fly B students remain idle throughout this phase. Step 2 also offers separately labeled recorded runs (always 288 rounds) for the timed pitch.
3. **Train Fly B students.** After Fly A finishes, click **Teach Fly B**. The two visible brains are Fly B With Teacher and Fly B Alone; a third matched random-message control runs in the background, with its score above and in the frozen comparison. The visible message strip shows Fly A's preference, measured DNge175 spikes and decoded preference, then the taught student's own reward and advice updates. Advice arrives after Fly B plays, so it can affect later choices. All three use matched initial conditions and opponent streams. Changing Fly B's length does not discard a trained Fly A. With the 6-round option, the run pauses at a genuine frozen checkpoint, and **Continue training to 288 rounds** resumes the same students.
4. **Disconnect and compare.** Every mode evaluates on the same 72 opponent rounds with teacher communication, exploration, and learning disabled. Frozen checkpoints run at the study rounds (0, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96, 288) that fit within the chosen length, plus the final round; Quick runs only the final test. The study's early-advantage criterion is reported only for 288-round runs. Side-by-side cards show wins, per-cue results, and the signed teacher-minus-alone difference. **Training rounds vs. wins** at the bottom charts Fly A's wins per 24-round block, the Fly B curves, and every finished live run (rounds trained against final score, with Pearson r once three runs use different lengths). Live seeds can tie or favor Fly B Alone; the archived multi-control studies provide the research evidence.

The main view is a live **hybrid simulated neural communication** demo. The cue-specific student readout is an added reward-trained controller with frozen KC-to-MBON synapses. It is not the original dopamine-only RPS policy or transfer between actual flies. Each brain image uses connectome cell positions: dim dots are anatomical positions and bright dots are recent simulated spikes from that specific fly. KC counts and MBON bars show measured simulated spikes; zero is displayed as zero. The DN counts are actual simulated spikes held in an engineered measurement buffer.

Live frozen checks show a bouncing bar in the sticky progress header and beside the scores; Fly A's bar also counts the 72 test games. Each score still uses all 72 games. Fly B's final score reuses its already completed final checkpoint, and frozen student trials stop simulating after both actions and rewards are recorded because the next trial resets neural state. These demo optimizations do not change opponent sequences, actions, rewards, or learning updates.

## Research evidence and limits

### New archived action-preference comparison

Research Evidence opens immediately below the two connectome views. Scroll to **Action-preference teaching** and select any of six archived students. The chart and exact five-arm table load from the saved initial and confirmation artifacts, with frozen, teacher-disconnected 72-round evaluations after 0, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96, and 288 student training rounds. Chart checkpoints are equally spaced; their labels show actual rounds. They are **not** online training wins or a newly executed live Challenge. The [Strategy A independent audit](action-preference-independent-audit.md) and [Strategy B independent audit](action-preference-strategy-b-independent-audit.md) explain the gates and limitations.

This separate opt-in mechanism trains Fly A on its own game rewards, freezes its controller, and records its MBON-driven preferred action. A fixed code stimulates DNge175 neurons 3141 and 3165; Fly B decodes **measured simulated spike counts** and applies a small auxiliary imitation update to its own cue-specific controller *after* its own reward update. Fly B's action on that round was already chosen before the message. KC→MBON synapses are frozen. This is **engineered hybrid simulated neural communication**, not natural biological plasticity or a transfer of teacher weights.

On fixed observable **Strategy A**, four reported trained students each reached at least **58/72 frozen wins by round 6**; the earliest control did so at round 12. Their early mean advantages over the strongest controls were **+11.75, +11, +8, +11.75 wins/72**. On fixed observable **Strategy B**, teachers scored **67/72 and 69/72** after own-reward training. Trained students scored **70/72 and 69/72** at round 6 and both finished **70/72 disconnected**, with early strongest-control advantages **+17 and +11 wins/72**. Every messaged training arm decoded **276/276** messages from simulated DN spikes. Silent Strategy B students also eventually reached **70/72**. The supported difference is early retained learning speed under these fixed rules, not universally higher final accuracy. The separate [Strategy B protocol](action-preference-strategy-b-protocol.md) and [initial](../data/rps_action_preference_strategy_b_initial.json) and [confirmation](../data/rps_action_preference_strategy_b_confirmation.json) artifacts contain the full controls.

The teacher preference tape was recorded from a frozen, reward-trained Fly A before student training; Fly A was not continuously learning alongside Fly B. These small cohorts do not establish performance on noisy, changing, or arbitrary custom patterns, nor biological brain-to-brain teaching. Earlier failed experiments remain valid. The live Challenge includes alone, trained-advice, and random-message students; a live run is not a new locked result and lacks the full archived controls.

### Neural spikes versus direct delivery

Inside **Research Evidence**, open **Neural spikes versus direct messaging** for the separate locked Strategy B ablation. Its two students received the same frozen teacher preference through measured DN spikes or as a direct digital message at the same update time; silence and scrambled neural messages were controls. Across both students, **552/552 trained neural messages decoded correctly**. Early mean frozen wins/72 at rounds 6, 12, 24, and 48 were **70 neural / 70 digital / 35.5 silent / 52.75 scrambled** for student 11017, and **69 / 69 / 33.75 / 64** for 11018. Neural and digital arms had identical training actions, rewards, learning updates, weights, and every frozen prediction; both finished 70/72 disconnected. The student selector shows each seven-point curve and its exact table. This demonstrates reliable spike transport of learned information, with **no observed advantage over direct delivery**. It does not establish that downstream DN wiring or the complete connectome performs the external imitation update. See the [causal pathway report](connectome-contribution-ablation-report.md) and [write-once result](../data/rps_connectome_contribution_ablation.json).

The main screen states the archived Strategy A learning-speed result and its control limitation together. Open **Research Evidence** for the [write-once five-arm result](../data/rps_teacher_specific_speed_initial.json): a seven-point frozen chart and exact table for trained, silent, untrained, shuffled, and matched generic advice. In that one run, the guided student first reached 58/72 wins at 12 training rounds and the silent student at 96. All three other advice arms also reached 58/72 at round 12, so the trained-teacher-specific gate failed. These archived curves are separate from the judge's live three-student chart.

The same section retains an older Strategy B neural replay with pretrained teacher 830 and students 851/852. Its generic-veto arm failed its rejection-count matching gate and remains labeled as such. The [lab notebook](lab-notebook.md), [research story](research-story.md), and [matched trial archive](../data/rps_teacher_guided_matched_veto.json) contain the full measurements.

The older proposal/veto experiments established message delivery, advice-driven action changes, own-reward student updates, and disconnected policies, but **did not establish a retained-learning advantage specific to trained-teacher information over all required controls**. The newer action-preference system did pass its separately locked early-speed gates on fixed Strategies A and B; it uses a different timing and an added imitation update. A positive difference in the live three-student comparison alone cannot establish the newer claim. The older eight-pair one-bit *cue* task averaged +2.75/24 against disconnected training, below its predefined +4/24 practical threshold; it is not an RPS result. The older quantized RPS message demo on the legacy page uses a different pathway from DNge175.

Custom patterns start at their first move and repeat continuously across training episodes; each frozen checkpoint restarts the same pattern with fresh neural/action streams. A deterministic custom cycle cannot supply an independent opponent *sequence*, so its frozen result tests a new simulation stream on the same rule. The pattern indicator classifies circular previous-move transitions, including the last-to-first step, as **Predictable from previous move** or **Experimental: pattern requires additional history for perfect prediction**. This is an information check, not a promise the learner will succeed. Patterns with fewer than three observed previous moves report absent cues as **not observed**. No pattern phase or correct action is supplied to either fly.

## 30-second explanation

“Synapse asks whether one simulated fly brain can help another learn Rock–Paper–Scissors faster. Fly A learns an opponent rule from its own game rewards. Its simulated sensory spikes feed an added controller, whose learned action preference is sent as measured spikes in two neurons to Fly B. Fly B updates its own controller from its own reward and the decoded message, then plays with the teacher disconnected. On two fixed rules, archived matched controls show faster early learning. A direct digital message worked equally well, so this is reliable neural transport in a hybrid simulator, not biological teaching.”

## Technical answer for judges

The project simulates a **6,900-neuron connectome subgraph** with real-wiring spiking dynamics. A public previous-opponent-move cue drives VPN neurons and produces KC/MBON sensory activity. Fly A's **external contextual-bandit readout** learns action values from its own RPS rewards; after training, its frozen MBON-driven highest-value action is encoded as a three-symbol pattern in simulated DNge175 neurons 3141/3165. Fly B measures their **actual 240–280 ms spike counts** in an engineered buffer and decodes one preference. Fly B first chooses and plays its own action, updates its own cue-specific value head from its own reward, and then applies a small auxiliary imitation update from the decoded preference. Its KC→MBON synaptic multipliers are frozen. Frozen checkpoints use separate copies with teacher messages, exploration, and learning disabled. The live Challenge runs this same protocol with taught, alone, and random-message students; two student brains are shown. The older one-bit proposal/veto rule did **not** pass its teacher-specific retained-learning comparison and is on the archive page.

### Likely questions

| Question | Short answer |
| --- | --- |
| Why use a fly brain? | It lets us test whether a constrained simulated neural signal can carry useful learned information between two separate agents. The connectome supplies sensory spikes and the DN transmission substrate; the added controllers do the RPS value and imitation learning. |
| How do you know teacher information matters? | On fixed Strategies A and B, trained preferences accelerated early **frozen, disconnected** learning against matched silent, untrained, shuffled, and generic controls. Shuffling the preference weakened the result. Some controls reached the same final ceiling. |
| Could this work without the connectome? | Direct digital delivery of the same teacher preferences matched the neural arm exactly in the locked Strategy B ablation. The current implementation uses simulated neural sensory features, but a causal need for particular connectome wiring has not been shown. |
| How do you prevent direct-label cheating? | Fly A was trained through its own game rewards; its message came from its frozen MBON-driven action values, not the hidden strategy or an injected correct-action label. In the neural arm, Fly B's imitation step received only the preference decoded from measured DN spikes. The digital arm deliberately bypassed spikes as a causal control. |
| Is this biological learning? | No. The neural activity is simulated, while the RPS reward and imitation controllers and spike-count buffer are engineered. KC→MBON dopamine plasticity is frozen in these studies. No actual flies teach one another here. |
| What is the application? | This is a research prototype for asking how much information a limited neural-like channel can transfer between independently learning agents. It is not a deployed predictor or evidence of clinical or biological transfer. |
