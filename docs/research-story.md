# Synapse research story: can one simulated fly brain teach another?

> Working research history, assembled from the experimental results shared in this conversation and the supplied *Fly Hero: technical notes and lab notebook*. This is a source for a future hackathon story, not a claim that the complete teaching task has succeeded. Where results are preliminary, they remain labelled preliminary. Check every number against the repository's raw files before publication.

## The question

Can a connectome-derived simulated fruit fly neural network learn a task, then help a second independently simulated network learn it using only limited neural activity—without copying learned synaptic weights, sending an explicit answer, or replacing the original dopamine-based learning rule?

The distinction matters. A teacher signal might affect a student's actions immediately without causing learning. A signal may encode the current cue instead of something learned. A decoding test with an engineered answer is not evidence that real activity from a trained teacher works. The project therefore uses controls for untrained sources, shuffled signals, disconnected communication, paired initial states, and independent random seeds.

## Background inherited from the original Fly Hero project

The original Fly Hero simulated a rhythm game with visual projection neuron inputs, sparse Kenyon cell (KC) activity, mushroom body output neurons (MBONs) used for action selection, and dopamine-gated KC-to-MBON weight updates. Its later model used the Janelia/Google male central nervous system connectome and included the ventral nerve cord: approximately 6,900 neurons, 990,102 connections, and 6,419,481 synapses, as described in the supplied technical notes. The original project reports learning on the rhythm task, with shuffled/random-wiring and dopamine-blocking controls. Those are **inherited Fly Hero findings**, not findings of the two-fly teaching extension.

The original technical notes also document a JavaScript eligibility-trace integer-array bug discovered during Python/JavaScript parity testing and a readout failure in a separate Deep-RL experiment. That earlier readout issue—information can exist in neural spikes but be unusable by a decision rule—is a useful historical parallel, not proof that the later teaching model has the same cause.

Biological limits: the simulation uses a simplified spiking neuron model, engineered VPN lanes and MBON action groups, an engineered interface between two separate network instances, and a simplified dopamine-plasticity rule. It does **not** establish that real fruit flies communicate learned strategies this way.

## Experiment chronology: problem → clue → intervention → result

### 1. Replace the rhythm game with rock–paper–scissors

**Goal.** Fly A learns an opponent's hidden pattern. Fly B should learn faster if it receives a limited signal derived from Fly A's neural activity.

**Early result.** Teacher and student learned to win around 60–61% of held-out rounds, but trained teacher signals did not improve Fly B's eventual performance. A simple predictor using the previous opponent move could reach approximately 94%, so the plateau was not the theoretical difficulty of that game.

**Clue.** One action was almost never selected. Synapses associated with that action received few useful updates. The selected teacher channel also carried little *additional* information once the student's existing observation was accounted for.

**Lesson.** Above-chance game performance does not establish teaching. A message has to be informative *and* learnable, and the student has to choose the actions required to get useful feedback.

### 2. Prove that the original teacher-to-student channel really delivers information

**Original mechanism.** Three teacher neurons were sampled, spike counts capped at two, and values mapped to VPN stimulation in Fly B. About 81.8% of noninitial messages were saturated at all-zero or all-maximum, closely tracking observations Fly B already had.

**Clue.** Messages produced neural spikes and eligibility traces, but the student's MBON readout favored one action. Even a deliberately informative oracle-like control failed to produce learned decoding.

**Intervention.** Build a separate balanced known-signal task and calibrate receiving targets, rather than immediately attributing the absence of teaching to the fly's intelligence.

**Result.** A locked calibrated VPN channel eventually supported 100% known-signal decoding across 10 confirmation seeds, with shuffled messages near 50%. This demonstrated potential for learnable communication under that diagnostic—not actual teaching or compatibility with normal task cues.

### 3. Simplify the game to two cues and two reward mappings

**Task.** Cue 0 or cue 1 is presented. Mapping A rewards cue 0→action 0 and cue 1→action 2. Mapping B reverses those choices. One episode has 24 rounds.

**Initial failure.** With cue inputs on VPN lanes 0 and 1, the fly learned the initial mapping but failed after reversal, including when trained on the reversed mapping from scratch. For one failed run, action 2 was selected on 69 of the final 72 rounds.

**Tested hypothesis.** Maybe it simply lacked exploration. Balanced training gave more action-0 rewards but frozen performance remained near chance.

**Clue and fix.** Cue 1 activated too few useful KCs through lane 1. Delivering cue 1 through VPN lane 2 instead, without changing the connectome or dopamine rule, made both mappings learnable. Three preliminary seeds reached roughly 89–96% frozen accuracy after reversal.

**Lesson.** Choice of input neurons changes which circuits can learn a particular association. An exploration intervention did not resolve poor input encoding.

### 4. Find useful teacher information

**Early source.** Neuron 2132 carried learning-associated activity about strategy phase, but its original 0.5 threshold often produced a nearly constant bit. A pretrained teacher did not improve the student's RPS performance over untrained or shuffled controls.

**Signal calibration.** Continuous neuron-2132 traces differed between learned mappings, yet a binary threshold hid much of the difference. A global threshold of 1.915, chosen on one calibration teacher and checked on a separate teacher, increased measured mapping information beyond cue from 0.144 to 0.396 bits. Cue-1 discrimination improved, cue-0 discrimination remained weak.

**Lesson.** Finding information inside a simulated brain and transmitting that information faithfully are different problems. Thresholding can discard the learned distinction.

### 5. Prevent the teacher signal from overwhelming the student's cue

**Failure.** Strong teacher VPN stimulation recruited far more KCs than the normal cue and reduced frozen cue accuracy dramatically (e.g. 22/24 cue only versus 8/24 with the original 300 Hz, 120 ms message in one comparison). Weaker, shorter, and nonoverlapping pulses reduced but did not eliminate interference; a weaker channel also failed a decoding control.

**Intervention.** Move communication to small existing KC groups and put the message before the task cue. A 0–40 ms message, 30 ms gap, and 70–240 ms cue preserved cue accuracy in one frozen probe (21/24 cue only, 22/24 with message), though this alone did not prove decoding.

**New failure.** KC messages produced action-specific MBON spikes during stimulation, but the action-specific response had faded by the 240 ms readout. Balanced cue-free decoding remained around chance even when learning weights changed.

**Diagnostic change.** Temporarily retain message-window MBON spike counts until the decision. On a one-seed known-signal test, frozen accuracy rose from 12/24 before training to 24/24 after training; disconnected and shuffled controls remained near chance. With the same readout, one cue-present diagnostic likewise reached 24/24 after training while preserving most cue accuracy.

**Caveat.** Retaining prior MBON counts is an *engineered change to action readout*, not an unchanged biological mechanism. It showed learnable decoding but did not establish that an actual teacher was helping.

### 6. Test real live teacher messages, and discover a design mismatch

**Live scheduling.** Fly A processes the current cue first, activity is sampled before its action/feedback, and Fly B receives that bit before processing its own cue. This avoids using future outcomes as messages.

**Result.** A fixed-mapping live pilot produced no convincing teaching advantage: correct trained and cue-matched shuffled messages yielded essentially the same early decisions and the trained student's later teacher-disconnected accuracy was not better than learning alone.

**Clue.** Within one fixed mapping, neuron 2132's bit was nearly determined by the cue already available to Fly B. The earlier source analysis had measured differences *between* trained mappings, but the live task did not vary the mapping. Thus the successful information test and failed teaching test were asking different questions.

**Fix to experiment design.** Interleave mappings A and B, balance the cues, and send actual saved neural messages from the corresponding trained teachers. Cue-matched shuffling must disrupt mapping information rather than merely reorder almost identical bits.

### 7. Demonstrate a narrow, reproducible learned response

**First mapping-interleaved test.** Correct trained signals contained about 0.39–0.42 bits of mapping information beyond the cue, while cue-matched shuffling reduced that to almost zero. One seed's student improved from 11/24 before training to 19/24 with correct signals after training; control conditions stayed near chance.

**Replication and necessary control.** Some new student seeds gave weaker results, and providing a correct message at evaluation could change an independently trained student's action without any message-based training. Therefore the relevant contrast became **correct-signal-trained versus disconnected-trained students, both evaluated with exactly the same held-out correct teacher message**.

**Strongest reproduced finding.** With three independently trained teachers (501–503), two new Fly B seeds each (601–606), correct-signal training exceeded disconnected training in all six paired comparisons. Mean advantage: +5.17 correct decisions out of 24, about +21.5 percentage points. The extra correct choices were overwhelmingly limited to mapping B / cue 1: 29 of the 31 additional correct decisions came from that case.

**Synaptic evidence.** Under the successful condition, action-0-associated KC→MBON synapses strengthened through the existing dopamine update path, including 18 message-0-to-action-0 connections reaching their weight cap in all six students. The student learned a message-dependent response; it did not learn both mappings generally, and it could not infer randomly interleaved mapping identity after messages were removed.

### 8. Explain why the other cases did not improve

**Clue.** For cue 0, neuron 2132 often sent the same bit under both mappings despite those mappings requiring opposite actions. The message-induced KC patterns overlapped heavily (weighted overlap 0.92). For cue 1, different mappings yielded distinct bits and distinct KC responses.

**Counterfactual.** On one frozen diagnostic, changing five ambiguous teacher bits (without retraining) raised mapping-A/cue-0 accuracy from 1/6 to 6/6. This was an artificial probe of receiving ability—not a real teacher result.

**Conclusion.** The narrow success was partly limited by what the selected source bit communicated. The student had learned to use a signal, but the teacher signal was ambiguous for a different cue/mapping case.

### 9. Try other neurons and a small population, without cheating by supplying the answer

**Single-neuron tests.** Neuron 2134 looked promising on teacher calibration seeds 501–502 but performed only 56.9% on held-out cue-0 discrimination for both teachers 503 and 504, below the 70% gate. Neuron 2132 worked very well for cue 1 but was weak or reversed for cue 0.

**Two source neurons.** Fixed bits from neurons 2132 and 2134 did not produce a stable cue-0 mapping interpretation on independent teachers. The pooled held-out cue-0 accuracy was about 52.8% under one locked analytical interpretation.

**Population analysis.** The continuous activity of a four-MBON group (2146, 2134, 2124, 2122) carried mapping information on both cues, with held-out analytical-decoder accuracy 68.1% for cue 0 and 81.3% for cue 1. The analytical decoder stayed outside the flies.

**Simple compression.** A two-bit encoding of 2146 and 2134, with thresholds fixed on calibration recordings, reached 77.1% and 84.7% held-out mapping discrimination for cues 0 and 1, respectively. Four bits carried more information but would demand a larger receiving channel.

**Lesson.** More informative recorded activity is not automatically easier for a simulated student to decode. A learned mapping can be represented across neurons without a reliable one-bit interpretation in every trained teacher.

### 10. The new two-bit channel: discover the difference between silent and active bit values

**Initial integration failure.** The two-bit channel activated distinct KC groups, and one frozen natural-message check preserved overall cue accuracy (20/24 versus 20/24), but messages changed individual decisions. A balanced XOR-like decoding task failed: the student chose action 2 on 23/24 held-out rounds. Independent-bit tests showed the 2134 bit was moderately learnable; the 2146 bit especially failed when its correct response was action 0.

**Mechanism probe.** For 2146 bit 0, the relevant KC group was silent, action-0 MBON spiking during the message was zero, and action 0 was chosen on only 4 of 96 training rounds. Bit 2134 produced action-0 MBON activity and far more rewarded action-0 updates. Balanced exploration alone increased opportunities for rewarded action 0 but did not fix 2146 decoding (16/24 normal versus 15/24 balanced frozen accuracy on seed 517).

**Targeted change.** Represent 2146 bit 0 by actively stimulating existing KCs 1319/2016 rather than silence, while retaining KCs 506/545 for bit 1. In seed 517, accuracy rose from 16/24 to 22/24, with bit-0 accuracy improving from 4/12 to 10/12 and rewarded action-0 updates from 4 to 53. Disconnected and shuffled stayed at 12/24. Two further Fly B seeds scored 21/24 and 23/24 under active encoding. One cue-trained frozen check was 20/24 with cues alone and 21/24 with natural messages; rare patterns were still under-sampled.

**Remaining failure at this stage.** When both bits were used together in a conditional two-bit task (cue 0 depending on 2146, cue 1 depending on 2134), three Fly B seeds reached 17/24, 21/24 and 14/24, against disconnected 16/24, 13/24 and 12/24. Cue 1 with message pattern `10` was correct only 1/9 times across seeds. The mechanism of this two-bit interaction had not yet been established; later diagnostics are recorded under New work.

## What the evidence currently supports

- The source connectome-derived simulations can learn the two-cue mappings under calibrated input encoding.
- Certain trained MBON signals contain learned information about mapping identity, and a compact two-bit source representation improves held-out mapping discrimination across cues.
- An engineered communication interface can carry activity between independent simulated networks and produce reward-dependent changes in the student.
- A **narrow learned, message-dependent response** was replicated across three independently trained teachers and six paired students, chiefly mapping B/cue 1, with an average +5.17/24 advantage over disconnected training when both groups were evaluated with the same correct messages.
- Active representation of a formerly silent zero-bit improves a particular decoding diagnostic, replicated in a few Fly B seeds.

## What the evidence does not yet support

- Reliable teaching of the **whole** two-cue/mapping game.
- Reliable two-bit *joint* decoding or an improvement across all cue/mapping cases.
- Learning a policy that identifies randomly changing mappings after the only mapping message is disconnected. Under that task design, disconnected accuracy near chance is expected.
- A biologically natural fly-to-fly communication pathway, or transfer of these results to physical robots.
- A claim that the original connectome or original action readout was unchanged in all diagnostics: the retained-MBON count readout and the between-brain stimulus interface are engineered choices.

## Possible honest project-story arc

1. **Question:** Can one simulated fruit fly teach another using only neural spikes?
2. **First obstacle:** Both could learn independently, but the teacher's message didn't help.
3. **Unexpected failure:** Signals reached neural circuits yet either carried redundant information or disrupted the sensory cues.
4. **Method:** Separate information content, delivery, interpretation, reinforcement learning, and generalization into different experiments with controls.
5. **Breakthrough:** An early pre-cue interface with retained MBON readout enabled known-signal decoding, and a mapping-interleaved test showed reproducible learning from *real trained teacher activity* in one particular case.
6. **Explanation of the limit:** One source bit was ambiguous for cue 0; more neurons provided information but the student couldn't reliably combine bits.
7. **Latest clue:** Representing a zero bit as active neural stimulation improved action-0 learning, but simultaneous two-bit decoding still fails for specific patterns.
8. **Ending (currently):** A measurable narrow transfer result, paired with a precise unresolved question—not a claim of complete teaching.

The deterministic three-cue opponent has a practical teaching ceiling: isolated cue heads can acquire a strong frozen action after one correct reward, and generic veto or always-reject advice can supply that reward without a trained teacher. ### Sparse RPS neural teaching: confirmation did not support a reduced-message advantage

A separately [locked study](sparse-neural-teaching-protocol.md) kept the successful Strategy B hybrid teacher/student system and sent the teacher's learned action preference on **276 (100%)**, **69 (25%)**, or **28 (10.14%)** of 276 eligible training rounds. Delivered preferences were decoded from real simulated DNge175 spike counts; silent rounds kept the student's own game reward learning and omitted imitation. The initial pair (18001/18002) passed teacher, receiver, and independent-learning prerequisites. Its 25% and 10% trained-message arms both passed the predefined early advantage, earlier proficiency, and final disconnected reliability gates against silence plus exposure-matched untrained, shuffled, and generic controls. This triggered the preselected independent pair (18003/18004), without tuning.

The second pair preserved valid neural transmission and learned a three-action disconnected policy, but **neither sparse budget confirmed the teacher-specific early advantage**. At 25%, trained and shuffled arms both averaged **64.25/72** frozen wins at rounds 6/12/24/48 and first reached 58/72 at round 12. At 10%, trained, shuffled, and generic arms all averaged **52.5/72** and first reached 58/72 at round 24. The 100% reference passed both pairs. All trained arms ended at **70/72** or **71/72** after disconnection, but final controls often caught up. Correct sparse messages carried more task information than controls, yet the controls sometimes learned just as quickly on this small deterministic task. Thus reduced-frequency neural messages can support retained learning in these runs, but a **reliable sparse teacher-specific advantage was not demonstrated**. No bandwidth or energy savings were measured, and the earlier digital ablation still shows no neural-channel superiority. The [report](sparse-neural-teaching-report.md) and [initial](../data/rps_sparse_neural_teaching_initial.json) / [confirmation](../data/rps_sparse_neural_teaching_confirmation.json) write-once artifacts preserve all curves, controls, and failed gates. The established Strategy A/B findings and demo were unchanged.

###  — Real recorded room sensors did not pass the raw task gate
