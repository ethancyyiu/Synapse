The result: teaching helps most when the rules change

Fifteen independent runs. Each Fly B first learned one rule (Rock → Paper → Scissors), then the opponent switched to the reverse (Rock → Scissors → Paper). Average games won out of 72, tested with Fly A disconnected and learning switched off.

At 48 rounds: taught Fly B averaged 68/72 wins. Its average lead over the strongest control in each run was +24.9/72. The advantage is learning speed after the switch.

Full write-up of each study, with confidence intervals and links

20-pair study: after 6 rounds, taught Fly Bs won +10.3/72 more than the best control (95% CI +7.3 to +13.6; 15 of 19 pairs). Alone students catch up by round 288.

Rule change (15-seed study): 48 rounds after the opponent switched rules, taught Fly Bs averaged 68/72, against 10 alone, 32 random messages, 33 shuffled, and 23 untrained teacher. Lead over the best control: +24.9/72 (95% CI +17.8 to +32.1); random messages matched the teacher on 1 of 15 seeds. At 96 rounds, alone averaged 35. Random and shuffled messages also beat learning alone (32 and 33 against 10), so part of the gap to "alone" comes from any message loosening the old habit. The teacher's own contribution is the +24.9 over the best control, not the 58 over alone.

Both use locked protocols and frozen tests with Fly A disconnected, with untrained-teacher, shuffled, random, and alone controls.

Out-of-date teacher (locked protocol, 15 new runs): a Fly A trained just as well, but on the old rule, gave Fly B no help. At 48 rounds, average wins of 72: taught by the new-rule Fly A 68; taught by the old-rule Fly A 1; alone 14. Difference +67.1 (95% CI +65.6 to +68.4). So the gain comes from what Fly A knows about the current rule, not from having a confident teacher. It also does harm: by 96 rounds the old-rule teacher held its students at 1 win while those learning alone reached 31 (30 fewer, 95% CI 21 to 39 fewer; 12 of 15 runs). An earlier 3-run pilot agreed. Protocol · results · pilot.

Can Fly B learn whom to trust? (locked protocol, 14 completed runs): mostly. Here Fly B lowers its trust in the teacher whenever it plays the advised move and does not win. With the old-rule Fly A, at 96 rounds it won 36 of 72 on average, against 1 without the check: +34.2 (95% CI +25.6 to +42.0). With the new-rule Fly A it kept the speed-up (67 against 68 at 48 rounds). It still ended 7.5 wins below learning alone (43; 95% CI 2.2 to 12.9 below), so this study failed its pass rule, which required no remaining harm. Protocol · results.

Two teachers who disagree (locked protocol, 14 completed runs): a narrow fail. Fly B heard a new-rule Fly A and an old-rule Fly A on every round and was not told which was which. Keeping a trust value for each, it ended at 70 of 72 at 96 rounds: the same score as hearing only the new-rule teacher, on all 14 runs, and 34 above learning alone (36). But a Fly B that followed both equally also reached 62 by then, so the lead from choosing was +8.4 (95% CI +3.8 to +13.6), just short of the +4 lower bound the protocol required. This study failed its pass rule. Earlier on, the gap was wider (48 rounds: 68 against 50; 24 rounds: 46 against 10), but those were not the locked test. Protocol · results.

A second task (locked protocol, 15 runs): passed, on the third test. We replaced Rock-Paper-Scissors with a fault-response task: one of three alarms in random order, three responses, and a change in which response fixes each fault. 48 rounds after the change, taught Fly Bs won 70 of 72 on average, against 44 with shuffled messages, 36 random, 25 with an untrained teacher, and 8 alone. Lead over the best control in each run: +19.0 (95% CI +11.8 to +26.2). On 4 of 15 runs a control came within 4 wins of the taught fly, and by 96 rounds the lead had shrunk to 6.3, so here too the gain is speed. What to hold against it: two 3-run pilots came first and both failed their rules. With a small change (one alarm), Fly B alone was perfect within 24 rounds and the teacher added nothing. With this large change, the taught fly led by 25, 0, and 25, and the pilot rule needed all three. We had said we would stop there; we reopened it as a 15-run test with the averaged rule the Rock-Paper-Scissors study used. Protocol · results · pilot 1 · pilot 2.

Our own simulator (locked protocol, 10 of 15 runs completed): passed. Everything above ran on the neural simulator that came with Fly Hero. We wrote a second one for this project (js/engine.js): the same published neuron model and the same connectome, but solved exactly at each step instead of approximately, so its spikes are not the same. We repeated the rule-change study on it with nothing re-tuned. In the 10 runs that completed, taught Fly Bs won 70 of 72 at 48 rounds, against 28 shuffled, 25 random, 24 with an untrained teacher, and 4 alone. Lead over the best control in each run: +35.0 (95% CI +28.2 to +41.4), and the taught fly led by more than 4 in every one. What to hold against it: on 5 of 15 runs Fly A did not reach its 58-of-72 bar on the new simulator (it scored 47 to 54) and the run stopped, which never happened on the original simulator; the protocol needed at least 10 completed runs and got exactly 10. Neurons are less active on the new simulator, and the learner settings were tuned for the old one. So the result holds when the teacher has learned, and the teacher learns less reliably. Follow-up (locked protocol, 15 new runs, teacher only): when we re-measured the two numbers that scale the learner's inputs on the new simulator, using no rewards or answers, Fly A reached its bar on 15 of 15 runs, against 12 of 15 with the old numbers on the same runs. The teaching study has not been repeated with the new numbers. Protocol · results. We wrote this simulator after reading the original, so it is a check on one codebase, not an independent replication. Protocol · results · pilot.

Research

How the system works, what it does and does not show, and the archived experiments behind it.

How it works

  1. Fly A learns. A simulated fruit-fly connectome plays Rock-Paper-Scissors. A small readout of its brain activity learns from its own wins and losses; the connectome's own synapses stay fixed.
  2. Fly A sends spikes, not weights. On each round it sends its preferred move as spikes on two descending neurons (DNge175) in Fly B's brain.
  3. Fly B learns for itself. Fly B plays its own move, learns from its own reward, and leans toward Fly A's suggestion. Then Fly A is disconnected and Fly B is tested alone.

Where this could lead

Real systems rarely share one model. When conditions change, a partner that has already adapted could help a differently built one catch up — sending a few bytes of advice, without handing over its model or its data. Three concrete scenarios where this matters today:

Fraud detection

Fraudsters change tactics. Bank A adapts. Bank B runs a different model — private data, incompatible architecture. Bank A sends "I would block this." Bank B adapts faster. Dal Pozzolo et al. 2018: fraud drifts continuously across 75M real transactions.

Hospital diagnostics

Sepsis criteria change. Hospital A updates. Hospital B can't receive it — HIPAA blocks data, different EHR blocks weight transfer. Hospital A sends a per-patient flag. Hospital B recalibrates. Finlayson et al. 2021: deployed clinical AI degrades as patients shift.

Mixed vehicle fleets

Construction zone reverses a street. Tesla learns it. Waymo hasn't — different OS, competitor IP. At the junction, Tesla sends "I now do X." Waymo adapts before its own data catches up. Open X-Embodiment 2023: cross-robot learning today needs one shared model.

Our result has four properties that make this possible:

  • Different buildsFly B's learner has a different structure from Fly A's, so nothing could be copied
  • Tiny messageunder 12 bytes in total before the test point
  • Nothing private sentonly "I would play this move", never weights or game history
  • Works after a change15 of 15 taught flies recovered within 48 rounds; 0 of 15 alone

What published research already shows

  • Advice works between differently built learners. A trained agent that may advise only a limited number of times still speeds up a student that uses a different learning method and a different view of the task (Torrey & Taylor 2013). This is the idea we test inside a connectome.
  • Sharing weights has two real problems. The standard way for separate sites to learn together averages their model weights, so every site must run the same model (McMahan et al. 2017). And the updates they share can be turned back into the private training data, pixel for pixel (Zhu et al. 2019). Sharing outputs instead of weights lets differently designed models learn together (Li & Wang 2019).
  • Rules really do change in deployed systems. Clinical AI models degrade when technology, patients, or behaviour shift (Finlayson et al. 2021). Fraud patterns drift over time in a three-year stream of 75 million card transactions (Dal Pozzolo et al. 2018).
  • Checking the adviser is an open problem. Recent work has students ask for advice only when they are unsure (Da Silva et al. 2020). Our own tests went the other way: Fly B learned from its wins how much to trust each teacher. That removed most, not all, of the harm from one out-of-date teacher. With a current and an out-of-date teacher together, it ended exactly where it would have with the current one alone, though following both equally got most of the way there too (Stats tab; both studies failed their strict pass rules).
  • Real flies and real brains do this. Fruit flies copy the mate choices and egg-laying sites of other flies, and the habits spread through groups (Danchin et al. 2018; Battesti et al. 2012). Rats have solved a task using brain activity transmitted from another rat (Pais-Vieira et al. 2013). These are different mechanisms from ours; they show the question is a biological one too.

What we do not claim

  • Not a new learning method. A teacher suggesting moves to a student is "action advising", a known idea in reinforcement learning (Torrey & Taylor 2013). What is new here is the test, not the method: (1) the advice travels as spikes on two neurons inside a simulated fly connectome; (2) it works when the student is built differently from the teacher (48 learned numbers against 13), so nothing could have been copied; (3) every claim was run against controls fixed in advance, including a teacher that is confident but out of date; and (4) we measured what happens when Fly B has to judge its teachers for itself, with one teacher and with two who disagree, and report where that fell short.
  • Spikes are a working channel, not a better one. Sending the same advice digitally worked equally well (archived ablation, 552/552 spike messages decoded). We show learned knowledge survives a neural channel, not that neurons beat a network cable.
  • Two simulators, one starting point. The demo and most studies run on the simulator that came with Fly Hero. Our own simulator reproduces the main result when the teacher learns, but the teacher failed to learn on 5 of 15 runs there, so we have not moved the demo to it.
  • It is a hybrid brain. The connectome senses and carries the message; a small learned readout picks the move, and Fly B's imitation step is engineered. The connectome and dopamine rule are unchanged.
  • Fly B trusts its teacher. It follows advice without checking it. In a locked 15-run study, a teacher trained on the old rule kept Fly B stuck at its starting score, 30 wins below learning alone by 96 rounds. A Fly B that learns from its own wins how much to trust its teacher got most of that back, but not all of it. Given a current and an out-of-date teacher at once, it ended level with having the current one alone (see the Stats tab).
  • Speed, not a higher ceiling. Students learning alone close the gap given enough rounds. The gain is adapting faster after a change.
What is this? Research notes and limits
  • Each simulated connectome produces sensory spikes; engineered controllers learn from game rewards.
  • In the archived action-preference study, Fly A’s learned preference traveled through measured DN spikes. Fly B updated its own controller from its reward and the decoded message, then played without Fly A.
  • On fixed Strategies A and B, trained advice sped early frozen learning against silent, untrained, shuffled, and generic controls. Direct digital delivery of the same preference matched the spike route; final accuracy was often the same.

The supported result is faster early retained learning in an engineered hybrid system on two fixed RPS rules.

The live Challenge below runs the same action-preference protocol as the archived study, including its engineered imitation update. A matched ablation found neural-spike delivery and direct digital delivery equally effective. A live run is a demonstration, not a new locked result, and these results do not establish biological teaching or arbitrary-opponent generalization. Older proposal/veto experiments failed their gate and are in the archive.

The broader question is whether independently trained agents can share useful learned information through a constrained channel. A future sensor-based benchmark might distinguish normal equipment operation, overheating, and excessive vibration. That task is proposed only; it has not been implemented, deployed, or measured here.
