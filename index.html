Our solution: advice, not data

We rebuilt the bank problem as a controlled experiment, inside two brains built from a real fruit-fly wiring diagram.

The bank problem, as an experiment

In the bankIn our experiment
The bank that adapted firstFly A, trained on the new rule
The credit union, built differentlyFly B, a different learner (48 numbers vs Fly A's 13), so nothing can be copied
A new scamThe opponent reverses its Rock-Paper-Scissors rule
"Block" or "allow"Fly A's preferred move, sent as spikes on two neurons

What we test: compact action advice between different learners, compared with controls fixed in advance (shuffled, random and out-of-date advice). The catch: the adviser must see the case it advises on, so this fits where it already sees the payment, such as a card network or the bank on the other side of a transfer.

The result: teaching helps most when the rules change

15 runs. Fly B learned Rock → Paper → Scissors, then the opponent reversed it. Average wins out of 72, tested with Fly A unplugged.

At 48 rounds: taught Fly B won 68/72, a lead of +24.9 over the strongest control in each run. The gain is speed after the switch.

Full write-up of each study, with confidence intervals and links

All locked in advance; Fly B is always tested with Fly A unplugged. Scores are wins out of 72.

Fixed rule (20 pairs): passed. After 6 rounds, taught Fly B led the best control by +10.3 (95% CI +7.3 to +13.6). Students alone catch up by round 288.

Rule change (15 runs): passed. At 48 rounds: taught 68, shuffled 33, random 32, untrained teacher 23, alone 10. Lead over the best control: +24.9 (95% CI +17.8 to +32.1). Any message helps a little, so the teacher's own effect is the +24.9, not the gap to alone.

Out-of-date teacher (15 runs): passed. A teacher trained on the old rule gave no help: 1 against 68 at 48 rounds, a difference of +67.1 (95% CI +65.6 to +68.4). By 96 rounds it held students 30 wins below learning alone, so a wrong teacher does harm. Protocol · results · pilot

Learning whom to trust (14 runs): failed. Lowering trust after bad advice recovered most of the harm: 36 against 1 at 96 rounds, +34.2 (95% CI +25.6 to +42.0). But it still ended 7.5 wins below learning alone, and the rule required no harm. Protocol · results

Two teachers who disagree (14 runs): narrow fail. Fly B ended level with hearing only the good teacher (70 of 72). Its lead over following both equally was +8.4 (95% CI +3.8 to +13.6), just under the required bar. Protocol · results

A second task (15 runs): passed, on the third try. An alarm-response task: taught 70, shuffled 44, alone 8 at 48 rounds. Lead: +19.0 (95% CI +11.8 to +26.2). Two 3-run pilots failed first; we reopened it as a full test. Protocol · results · pilot 1 · pilot 2

Our own simulator (10 of 15 runs): passed. Same neuron model and wiring, solved differently. Taught 70, alone 4 at 48 rounds. Lead: +35.0 (95% CI +28.2 to +41.4). But the teacher failed to learn on 5 runs; re-measured input settings fixed that (15 of 15), though teaching was not re-run. Protocol · results · pilot · follow-up

Research

How it works, why it matters, and what it does not show. Archived experiments are at the bottom.

How it works

  1. Fly A learns. A simulated fruit-fly connectome plays Rock-Paper-Scissors; a small readout of its brain learns from wins and losses. The wiring never changes.
  2. Fly A sends spikes, not weights. Each round, its preferred move travels as spikes on two neurons (DNge175) into Fly B's brain.
  3. Fly B learns for itself. It learns from its own rewards and leans toward the advice. Then Fly A is unplugged and Fly B is tested alone.

Why it matters

Who this affects, how big the problem is and how our approach differs are in the Usage and Simulation tabs.

What published research already shows

  • Fraud labels arrive late, and late labels hurt. Most card transactions are confirmed as fraud only days later, while fraud tactics keep changing (Dal Pozzolo et al. 2015; 2018). Models stuck waiting for delayed labels fall clearly behind (Csaba et al. 2024).
  • Sharing models or updates has limits. Federated learning needs every site to run the same model (McMahan et al. 2017), and shared updates can be turned back into private data (Zhu et al. 2019; Geiping et al. 2020).
  • Sharing outputs works across different models. Differently built models can learn from each other's predictions alone (Li & Wang 2019; Lin et al. 2020), and advice speeds up a differently built learner (Torrey & Taylor 2013).
  • Banks want to collaborate without sharing data. In a 2025 trial on synthetic transactions, 13 banks training together without sharing customer data detected fraud about twice as well as single banks (Swift 2025, self-reported).
  • Real flies learn from each other. Fruit flies copy other flies' choices, and the habits spread (Danchin et al. 2018; Battesti et al. 2012).

What we do not claim

  • Not a new learning method. A teacher suggesting moves is "action advising" (Torrey & Taylor 2013). What is new is the test: advice sent as spikes inside a fly connectome, to a differently built student, against controls fixed in advance.
  • Spikes are not better than a cable. Sending the same advice digitally worked just as well.
  • It is a hybrid brain. The connectome senses and carries the message; a small learned readout picks the move, and Fly B's imitation step is engineered.
  • Fly B trusts its teacher too much. An out-of-date teacher kept it stuck at its starting score. Letting Fly B judge the teacher by its own wins fixed most of that, not all.
  • Faster, not better in the end. Fly B learning alone catches up given enough rounds.
  • Small tasks, in simulation. Both confirmed tasks have three cues and three choices. The live Challenge is a demonstration, not a new locked result; failed older experiments are in the archive.

The problem

When fraud tactics change, the bank that sees it first adapts. Smaller banks find out last, and keep approving the scam until they do.

Who is affected

Credit unions and community banks

Small fraud teams, often under 10 analysts, and few confirmed fraud cases of their own to learn from.

Their members

While a new scam goes unflagged, people lose money, and the bank pays to investigate and refund.

The banks and networks that see fraud first

They want to help: 13 global banks trialled joint fraud detection in 2025. But they cannot hand over customer data or their models.

Why it matters

$12.5Bfraud losses reported to the FTC in 2024, up 25% in a year
70%+of credit unions and community banks saw fraud rise in 2025 (industry survey)
120 daysa cardholder has to dispute a charge, so confirmed fraud cases arrive late

Late labels are a known weak point: most transactions are confirmed as fraud only days later (Dal Pozzolo et al. 2018), and models waiting on delayed labels fall clearly behind (Csaba et al. 2024). Sources for the figures: FTC 2024 fraud data; Alloy State of Fraud 2025 (industry survey); Visa and Mastercard dispute rules; Swift 2025.

What happened on public card data?

We tested the idea on 284,807 real card transactions with ordinary machine-learning models, no fly brain: a bank's model sends a credit union's differently built model one flag per purchase.
